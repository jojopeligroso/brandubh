# Brandub in the Old Texts — a sourced reference file

*Working research compilation for site copy. Compiled 2026-07-29.*

**Purpose.** Every historical reference to the Irish board game **brandub / brandubh**
(and its named relatives **fidchell** and **búanfach / búanbach**) that survives in
texts **more than 600 years old** (composed on or before ~1426), with the Welsh
**gwyddbwyll** parallels alongside, plus (§5) the wider *Táin* frame the game sits in —
Cú Chulainn's lone defence at the ford, his non-soldier watchman Láeg, and Macha's curse
on Ulster. Quotes are given verbatim where corroborated, each with its text, date,
manuscript, translator, and source.

   Both are still *usable* on the site if framed honestly, and §1 shows how.

## §1. Two myths, and the better true stories underneath them

### Myth A — "In the Táin, Cú Chulainn plays the game."

**What the Táin actually says.** In the *Macgnímrada Con Culainn* (the "Boyhood Deeds"
section), the player is **King Conchobar**, not Cú Chulainn. Conchobar divides his
kingly day in three — *watching the boy-troop, playing fidchell, and drinking ale until
sleep takes him*. The boy hero **Sétanta / Cú Chulainn bursts onto the playing-field and
leaps clean over the fidchell board** in pursuit of the boy-troop; Conchobar catches him
by the wrist. So the board *does* appear at the hero's first entrance — but he **scatters
it**, he doesn't sit and play it. ✅ corroborated (multiple independent retellings of
O'Rahilly's *TBC Recension 1*).

> **Usable site line (honest, and arguably better copy):** *"The board is there at the
> hero's very first appearance — the boy Cú Chulainn vaults clean over the king's
> fidchell board as he storms the field at Emain Macha."*

Cú Chulainn's *personal* mastery of the games is real, but it comes from a **different
text** — *Tochmarc Emire* ("The Wooing of Emer"), which lists among his *búada* (gifts)
the "gift of fidchell" and "gift of búanfach." A list of accomplishments, no rules. ✅

The often-repeated picture of *"Cú Chulainn and his charioteer Láeg playing fidchell on
gold-and-silver pieces aboard the moving chariot, a symbol of his composure under
duress"* could **not** be tied to any verbatim medieval passage. It circulates only in
modern retellings. 🔶 **Do not present as medieval.**

### Myth B — "It's an ancient, thousand-year truth that the king always wins with perfect play."

**Not ancient, and not universal.**

- **No medieval source records the rules** of brandub or fidchell, and **none states
  which side wins.** The texts give the *pieces* (for brandub: a *branán* "king" + 4
  defenders against 8 attackers — see §2.5) and the special squares, nothing more. Every
  playable modern rule-set is a **reconstruction** from those literary hints plus
  archaeology plus the 18th-century Sámi *tablut* recorded by Linnaeus.
- The "king always wins" idea is a **modern** game-theoretic / hobby-community claim, and
  it is **ruleset-dependent, not a law of the game.** Cyningstan judges brandub
  "reasonably well balanced… favouring the king and defenders a little"; conversely an
  academic paper on a *forced-capture* variant finds the **attacker** has a forced win in
  three moves. Different reconstructions tip different ways.
- **This app's own evidence** (WTF reconstruction, our AI self-play): the **defender /
  King side wins the large majority** of well-played games, and deeper attacker search
  lengthens the game without changing the result. So *in these rules* the King is clearly
  favoured — which is exactly why the app scores matches as **side-swapping sets**.

> **Usable site line (defensible):** *"The medieval texts never wrote the rules down — so
> every tafl board today is a reconstruction. In the rules we ship, the King's side holds
> the advantage, which is why a fair match makes both players sit behind the King."*
> *(Avoid: "the ancients recorded that the king always wins." They didn't.)*

## §2. Primary references within the >600-year window (composed ≤ ~1426)

### 2.1 Sanas Cormaic (Cormac's Glossary) — *fidchell*
- **Date:** composition **c. 900** (attrib. Cormac mac Cuilennáin, †908), Old Irish.
  Manuscripts (all pre-1426): Book of Leinster, Yellow Book of Lecan, Book of Uí Maine.
- **What it attests:** the name and a folk-etymology of *fidchell* ("wood-sense" —
  requiring *ciall*, sense, and *fáth*, learning), and a **squared board with black and
  white men** (two colours / two sides). No rules, no winner.
- **Confidence:** 🔶 verify exact wording against Stokes/Meyer's edition.
- **Sources:** codecs.vanhamel.nl/Sanas_Cormaic · archive.org (Stokes ed., cu31924071173474)

### 2.2 Táin Bó Cúailnge — *Macgnímrada* (Boyhood Deeds) — *fidchell*
- **Date:** Recension 1 language 8th–9th c.; Book of Leinster recension 12th c.
  Manuscripts: Lebor na hUidre (c. 1106), Yellow Book of Lecan, Book of Leinster
  (TCD MS 1339, c. 1160). Within window.
- **Verbatim Irish** (Wikisource, after O'Rahilly): **"a trían ind laí oc déscin na
  macraide, a trían n-aill oc imbirth ḟidchille, a trían n-aill oc ól chorma conid gaib
  cotlad de."**
- **English:** *"a third of the day watching the boys, another third playing fidchell,
  and a third drinking ale until sleep took him"* (of **Conchobar**) — followed by the
  boy Cú Chulainn **leaping over the fidchell board.**
- **What it attests:** fidchell as the elite royal pastime; a real board with pieces that
  scatter. No rules, no winner; the player is the **king**, not the hero (see §1A).
- **Confidence:** ✅ corroborated (context and act); 🔶 verify the exact Irish string
  against O'Rahilly, *TBC Recension 1* (DIAS, 1976).
- **Sources:** celt.ucc.ie/published/T301035/ · wikisource "Mahagaja/Tain"

### 2.3 Tochmarc Emire (The Wooing of Emer) — *fidchell*, *búanfach* (+ brandub tradition)
- **Date:** earliest layer 8th c.; longer recension 10th–11th c. Mss incl. Rawlinson
  B 512. Within window.
- **What it attests:** among Cú Chulainn's *búada*: "the gift of fidchell, the gift of
  búanfach…" — the games as marks of the complete hero/noble. No rules, no winner.
- **Confidence:** ✅ (the gift-list); 🔶 verify exact translation (Kuno Meyer).
- **Sources:** celt.ucc.ie/published/T301021.html · en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tochmarc_Emire

### 2.4 Tochmarc Étaíne (The Wooing of Étaín) — the great *fidchell* match
- **Date:** language 8th–9th c. Manuscripts: Lebor na hUidre (c. 1106, partial); Yellow
  Book of Lecan (c. 1401, complete). Edited Best & Bergin. Within window.
- **What it attests:** the fullest scene of fidchell *being played* — **Midir** plays
  King **Eochu / Eochaid Airem** at Tara for rising stakes; Eochu keeps winning until
  Midir wins the decisive game and claims Étaín. It is about **wagers and stakes**, not
  rules or a structural "king wins." Prime material for the *board-game-as-fateful-contest*
  theme.
- **Confidence:** ✅ (episode); 🔶 verify any verbatim line.
- **Sources:** codecs.vanhamel.nl/Tochmarc_Étaíne · en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wooing_of_Etain

### 2.5 Acallam na Senórach (Colloquy of the Ancients) — **the brandub piece-inventory** ★
- **Date:** composition **c. 1200** (late Middle Irish) — ~826 years old, **within
  window.** *Note:* the surviving manuscripts are later (Book of Lismore, Laud Misc. 610,
  Rawlinson B 487, Franciscan A 4, all 15th–16th c.) — the *composition* is in-window, the
  *codices* are not.
- **English (Standish H. O'Grady, *Silva Gadelica* / *Colloquy with the Ancients*, 1892,
  from the Book of Lismore):** **"My famed brandub is in the mountain above Leitir Bhroin,
  five voiceless men of white silver and eight of red gold."**
- **What it attests:** the single most useful medieval datum on brandub — **thirteen
  pieces, five against eight, in two materials/colours** (silver vs gold). Still only an
  *inventory* of the men: **no board layout, no rules, no statement of who wins.**
- **Confidence:** ✅ English wording + translator corroborated across independent sources.
  🔶 the **original Irish** of the stanza was **not** seen — verify against Stokes,
  *Acallamh na Senórach* (*Irische Texte* IV.1, 1900) / CELT G303000.
- **Sources:** archive.org/details/silvagadelicaixx02ogra · iso.ucc.ie/Acallamh-senorach/ ·
  en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acallam_na_Senórach

### 2.6 Welsh — Breuddwyd Rhonabwy (The Dream of Rhonabwy) — *gwyddbwyll* ★
- **Date:** composition late 12th–early 13th c. Manuscript: Red Book of Hergest
  (c. 1382–1410). Within window.
- **What it attests:** the definitive **board-game-mirrors-battle** set-piece — **Arthur
  plays gwyddbwyll against Owain** while, move for move, Arthur's men and Owain's **ravens**
  fight and die around them. As each disaster is reported Arthur only says **"Gwarae dy
  wyddbwyll" — "Play your game."** No rules, but the richest literary use of the theme.
- **Confidence:** ✅ (scene and the "play your game" refrain); 🔶 verify exact Welsh/English.
- **Sources:** en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dream_of_Rhonabwy · oxfordreference.com

### 2.7 Welsh — other gwyddbwyll attestations
- **Breuddwyd Macsen (The Dream of Macsen Wledig)** and **Peredur fab Efrawg**, both
  12th–13th c., in the White Book of Rhydderch (c. 1350) and Red Book of Hergest
  (c. 1400). Within window. The Macsen dream reportedly gives gwyddbwyll a **silver board
  with gold men** (🔶 verbatim not confirmed).
- **Sources:** oxfordreference.com (Mabinogion entries)

### 2.8 Búanfach / búanbach
- Named in *Tochmarc Emire* (§2.3) and across Ulster-cycle material as a game "like
  draughts," companion to fidchell and brandub. **No medieval description of its board,
  pieces, or rules survives** — everything specific is modern inference. Within window as
  a *name only*.

## §3. Borderline / outside the window — handle with a caveat

### "Abair riom a Éire ógh" — the *branán* and the five "noble squares"
This bardic poem (poet **Maol Eoin Mac Raith**) is the source of the evocative image of
the **branán ("king"-piece) leaping onto the special central square**, and of the five
royal squares named as **Tara, Cashel, Crúachan (Croghan), Naas, and Aileach** — i.e. the
board as *Ireland in miniature* with the High King at the centre.

> Fragment (Mac White's translation): *"Advance thither, it will be a profitable step:
> leap up on that square, which is fitting for the Branán; the board is fittingly thine."*

**Window flag:** ⚠️ **treat as OUTSIDE / uncertain.** The only fixed date is its
manuscript, the **Book of O'Connor Donn (1631)** — outside the window. Mac White dated the
*poem* only loosely by style to bardic court-poetry (broadly **1200–1640**); it cannot be
shown to be pre-1426. **Do not cite it as a >600-year source** without a firmer date — but
it is fine as "later bardic tradition."
- **Source:** Mac White, *Éigse* 5 (unicorngarden.com/eigse/eigse06.htm); sluggerotoole.com

## §4. On game balance and "who wins" (for the balance/copy discussion)

- **Piece count / nominal balance:** brandub is **5 vs 8** — one *branán* (king) + four
  defenders against eight attackers. The defenders are deliberately outnumbered **two to
  one**, the conventional tafl valuation that *one defender ≈ two attackers*.
- **Effective balance is ruleset-dependent.** There is **no universal or ancient** verdict.
  Cyningstan: brandub "favouring the king and defenders a little"; a forced-capture variant
  (arXiv 2301.06127): **attacker** wins in three. The medieval texts are silent.
- **In the rules this app ships (WTF reconstruction):** self-play shows the **King's side
  is clearly favoured** — hence the side-swapping "set" scoring.

## §5. The hero at the ford — the Ferdia combat, Láeg the watchman, and Macha's curse

*Broader Táin context, for narrative site copy about the lone defence of Ulster. Same
confidence rules apply; the original Irish for this section could **not** be opened
in-environment (egress blocked), so it is flagged 🔶 with edition pointers rather than
reproduced from memory.*

### 5.1 The episode — *Comrac Fir Diad* ("The Combat of Ferdia")
- **Text/date:** the climactic single combat of the *Táin Bó Cúailnge*; fullest in the
  Book of Leinster recension (12th c.), Recension I language 8th–9th c. Within window.
- **What it is:** Cú Chulainn holds the ford of Muirthemne **alone** against Medb's army
  and, over three–four days, kills his own foster-brother **Ferdia** — they had trained
  together under Scáthach — with the *gáe bolga*. By night the two part as friends (shared
  fire, exchanged healing herbs, horses in one paddock) until the final day, when they
  camp apart.
- **Confidence:** ✅ episode corroborated; 🔶 verbatim Irish/English to be quoted from
  O'Rahilly.

### 5.2 Láeg mac Riangabra — the wakeful non-soldier ("night watchman")
- Cú Chulainn's **charioteer**, pointedly *not* a warrior: the one wakeful non-combatant at
  the ford through the nights of the combat. He keeps watch, names each oncoming champion
  and his weapons, hands the weapons across (floats the *gáe bolga* down the river), tends
  the wounds, and — on Cú Chulainn's own prior order — **rouses him with taunts** when he
  flags.
- The tradition also describes him keeping **"watch and ward of the four airts
  (directions)"** and as the man who **"won every other game of *fidchell* and *búanbach*
  from his master"** — the board-game thread and the watchman role meeting in one figure.
- **Secondary rouser (a different man):** **Súaltam**, Cú Chulainn's mortal father, who
  rides round Ulster trying to wake the stricken warriors — even his severed head keeps
  repeating the warning. Not the ford-companion; that is Láeg.
- **Confidence:** ✅ role corroborated; 🔶 the "four airts / won every game" descriptor
  needs pinning to a specific edition before it is published.
- **Source:** "'My only friend is my charioteer': Láeg mac Riangabra in *Táin Bó
  Cúailnge*" (Univ. of Cambridge repository); en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Láeg.

### 5.3 The spell on Ulster — Macha's curse (*Cess Noínden Ulad*)
- **Text/date:** *Noínden Ulad* ("The Debility of the Ulstermen"), 9th c. Mss: Book of
  Leinster, Yellow Book of Lecan, Book of Fermoy. Within window.
- **What it is:** **Macha**, forced while heavily pregnant to race the king's horses, wins,
  gives birth, and curses the men who heard her cry to be seized — *in their hour of
  greatest need, for nine generations* — by the pangs of a woman in childbirth. It is a
  **labour-pang debility, not a sleep spell.** Cú Chulainn is exempt (not reckoned "of the
  Ulaid" / his part-divine youth), as are women and children — which is also why a
  charioteer like Láeg is up while the warriors lie helpless.
- **Two corrections for copy accuracy:**
  - **Macha, not the Morrígan** — but Macha is routinely counted among the three
    war-goddesses (the *Morrígna*: Badb, Macha, and the Morrígan/Nemain), so "the Morrígan
    cursed Ulster" is a common loose conflation. The *specific* curse is Macha's.
  - **The Morrígan proper does appear at this ford**, in a different role: she offers Cú
    Chulainn her love, is refused, and attacks him mid-combat as an **eel, a she-wolf, then
    a red-eared heifer** (during his fight with **Loch**, not Ferdia); he wounds her in each
    form and later, unknowing, heals her.
- **Confidence:** ✅ corroborated; 🔶 original Irish of the curse to be quoted from CELT
  **G301025** / Hull, *Celtica* 8 (1968) / Meyer, *ZCP* 8 (1912).

## §6. Ready-to-use pull-quotes (verified enough to publish, framed honestly)

> **Brandub, thirteen pieces.** *"My famed brandub is in the mountain above Leitir Bhroin,
> five voiceless men of white silver and eight of red gold."* — *Acallam na Senórach*,
> c. 1200 (tr. Standish O'Grady, 1892).

> **The king's game.** In the *Táin*, King Conchobar spends a third of every day at
> fidchell; the boy Cú Chulainn makes his entrance by **leaping clean over the board.**

> **The board is the battle.** In the Welsh *Dream of Rhonabwy*, Arthur plays gwyddbwyll
> as his men and Owain's ravens fight around him, and to every report of slaughter he says
> only: **"Play your game."**

> **Wood-sense.** *Fidchell*, glossed in Cormac's Glossary (c. 900) as the game that "needs
> sense and learning to play it," on a squared board of black and white men.

> **The lone defence** *(paraphrase for narrative copy — not a translation).* By Macha's
> curse the men of Ulster lie gripped by the pangs of childbirth in their hour of need;
> only the boy Cú Chulainn — not reckoned among the Ulaid — holds the ford, and beside him
> his charioteer Láeg, who is no soldier and keeps the watch.

*(Attach a one-line provenance note wherever these appear, e.g. "translations as cited;
medieval spellings vary." The last item is a paraphrase and must not be presented as a
verbatim medieval quotation.)*

## §7. Verify-before-publishing checklist

1. Original **Irish** of the Acallam brandub stanza (Stokes, *Irische Texte* IV.1, 1900 /
   CELT G303000). English + translator already ✅.
2. Exact **Irish string** of the Táin/Conchobar fidchell line (O'Rahilly 1976).
3. **Sanas Cormaic** wording ("féth-ciall/fáth-ciall"; "four-cornered… black and white
   men") against Stokes/Meyer.
4. Welsh **Macsen** "silver board, gold men" verbatim.
5. A firm **date for "Abair riom a Éire ógh"** — decides in/out of the window.
6. Discard as **modern embellishment** unless a real medieval source turns up: "Cú
   Chulainn plays fidchell in the moving chariot," and the "blood spilled on the board
   between the king and Cú Chulainn" anecdote.
7. **Láeg's watchman descriptor** — locate the exact edition/§ for "watch and ward of the
   four airts" and "won every game of *fidchell* and *búanbach* from his master."
8. **Macha's curse** — original Irish + published translation from CELT **G301025** (Hull,
   *Celtica* 8, 1968; Meyer, *ZCP* 8, 1912).
9. **Comrac Fir Diad** — verbatim Irish/English of the nightly-truce passage (shared fire,
   healing herbs) and the *gáe bolga* climax, from O'Rahilly (DIAS).
